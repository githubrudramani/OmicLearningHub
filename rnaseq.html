Part 1 · History

How RNA-seq evolved

Each step changed what a single experiment can measure: one gene, then thousands, then every transcript, then every cell, then every cell in place. The statistics changed with it. limma started on microarrays and was adapted to counts.

1977

One gene at a time

Northern blot

RNA separated on a gel and probed for a single known gene. Slow, low throughput, but the first way to see expression levels directly.

1995

Thousands of genes

Microarrays and SAGE

cDNA microarrays (Schena et al.) and SAGE measured thousands of known transcripts at once. Arrays could only detect what was printed on them and lost accuracy at very low or very high expression.

2004

Statistics

limma and empirical Bayes

Gordon Smyth's limma "borrowed strength" across genes to stabilise variance estimates when you only have a few replicates. That idea is still at the heart of the pipeline below.

2008

Sequencing arrives

RNA-seq

Short-read sequencing of cDNA (Mortazavi et al. in mouse, Nagalakshmi et al. in yeast) counted transcripts directly. It needs no probes, has a wider dynamic range, and finds novel transcripts and splice junctions.

2010

Count statistics

edgeR, DESeq and TMM

Counts are not continuous, so new models used the negative binomial distribution. Robinson and Oshlack introduced TMM normalisation, showing that dividing by library size alone gives biased results.

2009–13

Resolution

Single-cell RNA-seq

Tang et al. sequenced a single cell's transcriptome in 2009. By 2013 Nature Methods named single-cell sequencing its Method of the Year. The STAR aligner (2013) made fast spliced alignment routine.

2014

Statistics

voom and DESeq2

voom (Law et al.) converted counts to log-CPM with precision weights, so limma's linear models could be used on RNA-seq. DESeq2 was published the same year. Both are still standard.

2015–17

Scale and speed

Droplets and pseudo-alignment

Drop-seq and inDrop, then the 10x Chromium, profiled thousands of cells per run. kallisto and Salmon quantified transcripts in minutes, without full alignment.

2016–20

Location and length

Spatial and long-read RNA

Spatial transcriptomics (Ståhl et al., 2016) kept tissue position; Nature Methods made it Method of the Year 2020. Nanopore direct RNA and PacBio Iso-Seq read full-length isoforms. nf-core made pipelines reproducible across clusters and clouds.

2023–26

Now

Multi-million cells, isoforms and models

Long-read isoform quantification is affordable at scale, single-cell atlases reach tens of millions of cells, and spatial panels now cover thousands of genes. Bulk RNA-seq is still the most common design for comparing conditions, because it is cheap, deep and well understood.

Part 3 · Concept

Why TMM normalisation matters

A sequencer gives each sample a fixed budget of reads. If a few genes become hugely expressed in one sample, they use up a larger share of that budget, and every other gene gets fewer reads, even though its real expression did not change. Dividing by total library size (CPM) does not fix this, so unchanged genes look down-regulated.

TMM (trimmed mean of M-values) assumes most genes are not differentially expressed. For each sample against a reference sample it:

  1. computes each gene's log-ratio M and average abundance A;
  2. trims the most extreme 30% of M values and 5% of A values;
  3. takes a precision-weighted mean of what is left;
  4. turns that into a scaling factor, so the effective library size is lib.size × norm.factor.

Try it: increase the share of strongly up-regulated genes and compare the two modes. With CPM, the cloud of unchanged grey genes slides below zero. TMM moves it back to zero.

Now push the slider past about 30%. TMM fails too, because its core assumption (most genes unchanged) no longer holds. Global shifts like this happen in some experiments, such as transcriptional amplification by MYC. They need spike-in controls rather than TMM.

Upstream

From FASTQ files to a count matrix

limma-voom starts from a gene-by-sample table of raw read counts. For your own data, produce it with nf-core/rnaseq: trimming, STAR alignment, Salmon quantification and full QC (FastQC, RSeQC, MultiQC) in one command. It runs locally, on a cluster, or on AWS.

Shell
# samplesheet.csv: sample,fastq_1,fastq_2,strandedness
nextflow run nf-core/rnaseq -r 3.19.0 \
  -profile docker \
  --input samplesheet.csv \
  --genome GRCm39 \
  --aligner star_salmon \
  --outdir results

# counts for limma: results/star_salmon/salmon.merged.gene_counts.tsv
# on AWS: add  -profile awsbatch  or launch the same workflow on AWS HealthOmics
Don'tNever feed TPM or FPKM values into limma or edgeR. They need raw counts. With Salmon output, import via tximport(..., countsFromAbundance = "lengthScaledTPM"), which gives count-scale values that limma-voom accepts.
VersionPin the pipeline with -r for reproducibility. Check the latest release on the nf-core website before you run.

Metadata

Add sample groups, batch and gene annotation

The cell type is the biology. The sequencing lane is a technical batch you will put in the model. Gene symbols make results readable.

R
group <- factor(c("LP","ML","Basal","Basal","ML","LP","Basal","ML","LP"))
lane  <- factor(rep(c("L004", "L006", "L008"), c(3, 4, 2)))
x$samples$group <- group
x$samples$lane  <- lane

genes <- AnnotationDbi::select(org.Mm.eg.db, keys = rownames(x),
                               columns = c("SYMBOL", "GENENAME"), keytype = "ENTREZID")
genes <- genes[!duplicated(genes$ENTREZID), ]
x$genes <- genes
x$samples
Design checkEvery group appears on more than one lane, so lane and cell type are not confounded. If a batch matched a group exactly, no statistical method could separate them.
05

Filtering

Remove lowly expressed genes

Genes with almost no reads cannot be tested and add noise to the mean-variance trend. filterByExpr() keeps genes with enough counts in at least as many samples as the smallest group, scaled to library size.

R
table(rowSums(x$counts == 0) == 9)            # genes with zero counts everywhere

keep <- filterByExpr(x, group = group)
x <- x[keep, , keep.lib.sizes = FALSE]        # recalculate library sizes
dim(x)

# density of log-CPM before vs after filtering
lcpm <- cpm(x, log = TRUE)
plotDensities(lcpm, legend = FALSE, main = "After filtering")

06

Normalisation

TMM normalisation

calcNormFactors() computes one factor per sample. It does not change the counts; it stores factors that every downstream function (cpm, voom) uses automatically. Factors are scaled so they multiply to 1.

R
x <- calcNormFactors(x, method = "TMM")
round(x$samples$norm.factors, 3)
x$samples$lib.size * x$samples$norm.factors   # effective library sizes

# teaching exercise: distort two samples and watch TMM correct them
x2 <- x
x2$samples$norm.factors <- 1
x2$counts[, 1] <- ceiling(x2$counts[, 1] * 0.05)
x2$counts[, 2] <- x2$counts[, 2] * 5
par(mfrow = c(1, 2))
boxplot(cpm(x2, log = TRUE), las = 2, main = "Unnormalised")
x2 <- calcNormFactors(x2)
boxplot(cpm(x2, log = TRUE), las = 2, main = "TMM-normalised")
[1] 0.894 1.025 1.046 1.046 1.016 0.922 0.996 1.086 0.984
Read itThese factors are all close to 1, so composition bias is mild here. Factors outside about 0.5–2 point to a problem sample, very different RNA composition, or contamination. Investigate before you continue.
NewerFor data with many zeros (low-input or degraded samples), method = "TMMwsp" (TMM with singleton pairing) is more stable. In edgeR 4, normLibSizes() is the new name for calcNormFactors(); both work.

Model

Design matrix and contrasts

The design has one column per cell type (no intercept) plus lane as a batch term. Contrasts then name the comparisons you care about.

R
design <- model.matrix(~ 0 + group + lane)
colnames(design) <- gsub("group", "", colnames(design))

contr.matrix <- makeContrasts(
  BasalvsLP = Basal - LP,
  BasalvsML = Basal - ML,
  LPvsML    = LP - ML,
  levels = colnames(design))
contr.matrix
BatchModel batch in the design, as here. removeBatchEffect() is only for plots and heatmaps; never test DE on batch-removed values.

Core analysis

voom, linear model and empirical Bayes

voom converts counts to log-CPM using the TMM-adjusted library sizes. It then estimates how variance depends on expression level and gives each observation a precision weight. limma fits a weighted linear model per gene, and eBayes shrinks gene variances towards a common trend.

R
v <- voom(x, design, plot = TRUE)          # mean–variance trend should be smooth
vfit <- lmFit(v, design)
vfit <- contrasts.fit(vfit, contrasts = contr.matrix)
efit <- eBayes(vfit)
plotSA(efit, main = "Final model: mean-variance trend")
summary(decideTests(efit))

Results

Rank genes, apply a fold-change threshold and plot

With thousands of significant genes, focus on large effects. treat() tests whether the fold change is above a threshold, which is stricter and more honest than filtering the results by log fold change after the test.

R
tfit <- treat(vfit, lfc = 1)                 # |log2FC| > 1, i.e. more than 2-fold
dt <- decideTests(tfit)
summary(dt)
basal.vs.lp <- topTreat(tfit, coef = 1, n = Inf)
head(basal.vs.lp[, c("SYMBOL", "logFC", "AveExpr", "adj.P.Val")])

plotMD(tfit, column = 1, status = dt[, 1], main = colnames(tfit)[1], xlim = c(-8, 13))
glimmaMA(tfit, dge = x, coef = 1)              # interactive MD plot + per-gene counts

# volcano plot
tt <- topTable(efit, coef = "BasalvsLP", n = Inf)
tt$status <- ifelse(tt$adj.P.Val < 0.05 & tt$logFC > 1, "Up",

Biology · ranked GSEA

GSEA with MSigDB: all genes ranked by the moderated t

Rank every tested gene by limma's moderated t-statistic, which combines fold change, variance and sample size. Then ask whether a gene set's members pile up at the top (up-regulated) or the bottom (down-regulated) of the list. No cut-off is needed, so GSEA detects pathways where many genes shift a little, even if none passes FDR on its own.

R · ranks + gene sets
library(fgsea); library(msigdbr); library(data.table)
# MSigDB collections (msigdbr ≥ 10: collection/subcollection; older: category/subcategory)
# recent msigdbr downloads the gene sets on first use, so that run needs internet
hall <- msigdbr(species = "Mus musculus", collection = "H")
reac <- msigdbr(species = "Mus musculus", collection = "C2", subcollection = "CP:REACTOME")
gobp <- msigdbr(species = "Mus musculus", collection = "C5", subcollection = "GO:BP")
pathways <- split(hall$gene_symbol, hall$gs_name)

# rank ALL tested genes by the moderated t (from eBayes, step 09)
tt <- topTable(efit, coef = "BasalvsLP", number = Inf)
tt <- tt[!is.na(tt$SYMBOL), ]
tt <- tt[order(-abs(tt$t)), ]; tt <- tt[!duplicated(tt$SYMBOL), ]   # one value per symbol
ranks <- sort(setNames(tt$t, tt$SYMBOL), decreasing = TRUE)
R · run GSEA
gsea_all <- fgsea(pathways, ranks, minSize = 15, maxSize = 500)
gsea_all <- gsea_all[order(padj, -abs(NES))]
head(gsea_all[, .(pathway, NES, padj, size)], 10)
fwrite(gsea_all, "BasalvsLP_Hallmark_GSEA_allgenes.tsv", sep = "\t", sep2 = c("", ",", ""))

# running-score plot for the top set
plotEnrichment(pathways[[gsea_all$pathway[1]]], ranks) + ggplot2::ggtitle(gsea_all$pathway[1])

# clusterProfiler alternative (same idea, different API)
# clusterProfiler::GSEA(ranks, TERM2GENE = hall[, c("gs_name", "gene_symbol")], eps = 0)
Read itNES > 0: the set is enriched among genes up in Basal; NES < 0: enriched among genes up in LP. Use padj (FDR) < 0.05. The leadingEdge column lists the genes driving the score, which are the ones to plot and follow up.
Why t, not logFC?Ranking by logFC alone lets noisy, low-count genes with large fold changes dominate. The moderated t down-weights them, so it is the usual choice after limma.

Biology · cut-off based

Significant genes only (adj. P < 0.05): ORA and restricted GSEA

Sometimes you want to ask what your DE gene list is about. With a cut-off list, the standard test is over-representation analysis (ORA). Are up-regulated (or down-regulated) genes found in a set more often than chance, compared with all tested genes? Run up and down separately; pooling them hides direction.

R · ORA of significant genes
library(clusterProfiler)
t2g      <- hall[, c("gs_name", "gene_symbol")]
universe <- names(ranks)                                  # every tested gene, not the genome
sig_up   <- tt$SYMBOL[tt$adj.P.Val < 0.05 & tt$logFC > 0]
sig_down <- tt$SYMBOL[tt$adj.P.Val < 0.05 & tt$logFC < 0]

ora_up   <- enricher(sig_up,   TERM2GENE = t2g, universe = universe,
                     minGSSize = 15, maxGSSize = 500)
ora_down <- enricher(sig_down, TERM2GENE = t2g, universe = universe,
                     minGSSize = 15, maxGSSize = 500)
head(as.data.frame(ora_up)[, c("ID", "GeneRatio", "BgRatio", "p.adjust")])
dotplot(ora_up, showCategory = 15) + ggplot2::ggtitle("Up in Basal: Hallmark ORA")
R · GSEA restricted to significant genes
ranks_sig <- ranks[names(ranks) %in% c(sig_up, sig_down)]
gsea_sig  <- fgsea(pathways, ranks_sig, minSize = 10, maxSize = 500)
gsea_sig[order(padj)][1:10, .(pathway, NES, padj, size)]
Read with careWhen every gene in the list is already significant, GSEA no longer asks "is this pathway changed?". It asks "do this pathway's DE genes lean up or down?". Sets also need ≥ 10 significant members, so many drop out. Use it as a direction summary next to ORA, not as a replacement for the all-genes GSEA.

Glossary

CPM
Counts per million: counts divided by library size × 10⁶. With TMM, the effective (normalised) library size is used.
TMM
Trimmed mean of M-values: a scaling factor that corrects for differences in RNA composition between samples.
M / A values
M is the log₂ ratio between two samples; A is the average log₂ expression. Together they form an MA plot.
voom
Variance modelling at the observation level: turns counts into log-CPM with precision weights for limma.
Empirical Bayes
Shrinks each gene's variance estimate towards a common trend, giving stable tests with few replicates.
Contrast
A named comparison between model coefficients, for example Basal − LP.
FDR
False discovery rate: the expected share of false positives among genes called significant (Benjamini–Hochberg).
treat
Tests whether the fold change exceeds a threshold, not just whether it differs from zero.
